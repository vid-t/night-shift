A small language model watches one control loop of a plant and says when it is under attack.

It reads 300 seconds of signals, such as a boiler's water level and its valve. Code adds one hint line per signal, stating a number such as its largest one-second jump. The model answers attack or normal, names the second the attack began, and writes one sentence.

On the test set, run once, gradient boosting, a classic statistical method, is slightly ahead: 54 of a perfect 82, against 51 for three of our models voting and 50 for seven. Chance alone could erase that gap: on a redrawn test set, our vote is level or ahead about one time in three. Our model adds what gradient boosting does not give: the second the attack began, and a sentence a person can check.

A huge general model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, is far behind both. Asked cold, it calls about half of all normal windows an attack.

On the test set, gradient boosting is slightly ahead of our best: 54 against 51 and 50.

Score = attack windows caught minus false alarms, so calling everything an attack does not pay. The test set holds 1,536 windows: 82 attack windows from 56 attacks, and 1,454 normal ones. Perfect is 82. We fixed both designs, the voters and the scoring before the run, and ran it once.

The check-set lead did not hold. On the check set that guided the design, seven models voting scored 90 of 144 (63%), gradient boosting 82 (57%). On the test set: 50 of 82 (61%) against 54 (66%). Our vote held its level. Gradient boosting did better on fresh data.

3 points is inside chance. The test set covers 256 stretches of 300 seconds, each seen through six loops. Gradient boosting and our three-model vote score differently on only 29 of them: 16 go to gradient boosting, 13 to our vote. We also drew the test set again 20,000 times. In nine draws of ten the gap lies between −6 and +12, and our vote is level or ahead in about a third of the draws. The test set is also not fully fresh: see the limits.

A huge general model catches almost every attack, because it calls a third to a half of all normal windows an attack.

We asked Kimi K3.1 Pro about 811 of the test windows: all 82 attack windows and half of each loop's normal windows (729). Twice: cold, with the task and the raw numbers, then with our hints and six solved examples. It was not trained for this plant, so it does not know what normal looks like here.

Thinking did not fix it. On 58 windows (28 attack, 30 normal), Kimi cold caught 27 with 20 false alarms with thinking off, and 25 with 15 with thinking on (low), at four times the price. Gradient boosting caught 25 with 0 false alarms; our votes 20 and 21 with 0.

A limit of this comparison: Kimi got one wording, no long thinking, and numbers as text. That is what you get if you just ask a huge model, not a contest of equals.

      What worked

      • Short hints in the text. Code computes one or two numbers per signal and writes them next to the curve. On the check set, runs with hints average 73.1; the same views without them average 56.5.
      • Voting over repeats, to steady the score. On the check set, seven repeats of one design score 90 together; alone they score 63 to 86. On the test set the vote scores 50; repeats of its design alone score 39 to 50.
      • Keeping the last training pass instead of the pass with the lowest loss. A pass is one run through the training data; loss is the model's error on held-out check rows. The last pass was the best in all 8 runs that scored every pass.
      • A start second and a sentence. On the test set, the votes name the start within 30 seconds for 30 of 56 attacks, against 26 for a constant guess. Gradient boosting gives neither.

      What failed

      • Beating gradient boosting on fresh data. The check-set lead (90 against 82) became 50 against 54 on the test set.
      • More attack examples in training. At half attacks the check-set score fell to −3: far more false alarms for a few more catches.
      • Two blind spots no design fixed. On the check set: water-level attacks without a valve jump, and 12 boiler-temperature windows from a dataset version whose temperature attacks training never saw.

      A small model trained on the plant beats a huge general one, comes a close second to gradient boosting, and adds when the attack began.

      1. Training on the plant beats size. On the same 811 windows our votes score 59 and 57. Kimi K3.1 Pro scores −181 with our hints and −284 cold, because it calls a third to a half of all normal windows an attack. Each of our models is small, about 1 billion parameters, and trains in about an hour for under a dollar.

      2. On detection, gradient boosting is 3 to 4 points ahead, and that is inside chance. It and our three-model vote score differently on 29 of the test set's 256 stretches of time: 16 go to gradient boosting, 13 to our vote. During the night, on the check set we used to choose designs, seven of our models voting led 90 to 82. That lead did not carry over. Choosing on the check set likely flattered our designs there. Gradient boosting ran with default settings and still came out ahead.

      3. What it adds: when the attack began, and a sentence a person can check. An alarm alone does not tell an operator when or what. Either vote names the start within 30 seconds for 30 of the 56 attacks, where a fixed guess gets 26. That beats a fixed guess, but not by much. We did not measure how often the sentence is right. Gradient boosting gives no start and no sentence.

      4. Two weaknesses set the next step. The model leans on hints that code computes, more than on the curve. And one model alone is a gamble: retrained from a new random start, one design scores 42 to 52 of 82, the other 39 to 50. What we would try next, not tested here: let gradient boosting raise the alarm and let the language model explain it.

      What this does not show

      • The test set is not fully fresh. We scored earlier models and both baselines on it before this night, and chose the starting design after seeing those scores. The night's 64 runs never touched it.
      • 56 attacks are few. The test set cannot separate 54 from 51. Our redraw also understates the spread: the windows come from four recordings, and the three other votes we could have formed from the same four models score 48, 49 and 52.
      • The explorer, the curve check and the night use the check set. We chose the designs, the hints and the vote by looking at it, so those numbers are optimistic. It is small too: 144 attack windows from 15 attacks.
      • About 36 of the 144 check-set attack windows were out of reach for every design of the night. 24 are water-level windows with no valve jump. 12 are boiler-temperature windows from a dataset version whose temperature attacks training never saw.
      • It leans on computed hints. Blank curves leave most attack calls in place. With the test seconds shuffled, the hint-heavy design raises 395 to 677 false alarms of 1,454. The simpler design stays calm and loses about half its catches.
      • One model is a gamble. On the test set, one model of the simpler design scores 42, 52, 52, 43 over four repeats. On the check set a vote narrows that spread, at three or seven times the answering work.
      • The huge-model comparison is not a contest of equals. Kimi got one wording, no long thinking, and numbers as text.
      • It is a research demo, not a deployed detector. HAI is a lab-scale plant with recorded attacks, not a live site.
